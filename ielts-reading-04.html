02 · Academic reading · 13 min

When the night became a public resource

An original IELTS-style practice text. Read quickly once for the argument, then return to exact sentences when answering.

A. For generations, city lighting was treated mainly as a technical problem: how could streets become brighter without becoming too expensive? Today the question is more complicated. Research into artificial light at night, often abbreviated to ALAN, suggests that the location, colour and timing of a lamp may matter as much as its brightness. The challenge is not to make cities dark. It is to decide where light genuinely helps people and where it unintentionally changes the behaviour of other species.

B. Consider the night journey of a migrating bird. Many species use several cues, including the position of stars and the Earth's magnetic field, to maintain direction. Strongly illuminated buildings can disturb this process, particularly during low cloud or fog. Birds may circle a bright structure repeatedly, using energy they need for migration. Researchers are careful, however, not to claim that every bird near a lamp is disorientated. Wind, weather and the surrounding landscape influence what observers see, so a single night of observations cannot establish a general rule.

C. On a different coast, newly hatched sea turtles usually move towards the brighter horizon over the ocean. Lights from hotels and roads may compete with that natural signal, sending hatchlings inland instead. Here, colour is important: some shorter-wavelength lighting attracts or confuses wildlife more strongly than carefully selected longer-wavelength alternatives. Yet switching every bulb to amber is not a universal solution. A lamp's direction and the number of hours it operates also affect the amount of light reaching sensitive habitats.

D. In a fictional case study used in urban-planning classes, the town of Bellhaven compared three waterfront streets over six weeks. Street One kept its existing white lamps. Street Two installed shielded lamps that directed light downwards. Street Three used the same shields but dimmed the lamps after midnight. Volunteers counted visible birds, while engineers measured light spilling above roof level. The largest reduction in upward light was recorded on Street Three. Nevertheless, the bird counts varied considerably from week to week, and the researchers refused to attribute that variation solely to the lighting changes.

E. An unexpected result emerged from interviews with residents. Several people assumed that brighter light always made them feel safer, but once they walked along the test streets, many preferred the shielded lamps. They explained that reduced glare made faces, steps and the edges of the pavement easier to distinguish. This finding concerned perceived visibility, not a measured reduction in crime. The research team explicitly warned the council against presenting survey preferences as proof that any particular design prevented accidents.

F. Bellhaven's final recommendation was therefore a flexible lighting policy rather than a single bulb for every street. Busy crossings would retain adequate illumination, while empty waterfront paths could be dimmed during low-use hours. Lamps near nesting beaches would be shielded and selected with local wildlife in mind. Crucially, the proposal included follow-up monitoring: without it, officials could not know whether their choices worked once weather patterns and public behaviour changed. The broader lesson is that responsible lighting is less about choosing between people and nature than about testing where, when and why illumination is necessary.
