More Than Meets the Eye

First impressions, identity and the assumptions we make about other people.

WARM-UP · 6 MIN

Would you trust your first impression?

Look at these two situations. What assumptions might people make? Why could those assumptions be wrong?

👔A quiet new colleague

They rarely speak in meetings but consistently deliver excellent work.

🎸A musician with a serious day job

Someone performs at small concerts on weekends but works as a financial analyst.

1How quickly do you usually form an opinion about a new person?
2Have you ever been completely wrong about somebody?
3Do you think first impressions are useful or misleading?
Teacher notes

6 minutes. Encourage examples and follow-up questions. Do not correct every mistake. Listen for overly confident conclusions; they create a natural bridge to hedging and speculation.

VOCABULARY · 8 MIN

The language of assumptions

Match each expression with its meaning, then use at least three expressions to describe a real situation.

Make it personal: Tell your teacher about a time you jumped to conclusions, or when someone got the wrong impression of you.

READING & DISCUSSION · 10 MIN

The colleague nobody understood

Read the story and find out how one team's opinion changed.

When Maya joined a software company in Manchester, her new colleagues found her difficult to read. She rarely joined conversations in the kitchen, kept her camera off during optional online meetings and often left the office without saying goodbye.

Within a few weeks, some teammates had started describing her as unfriendly. One colleague even suggested that Maya probably didn't enjoy working with people. Nobody had actually asked her about it.

Then a demanding project ran into trouble. Maya volunteered to coordinate the team. She listened carefully to everyone's concerns, handled difficult client conversations calmly and made sure quieter colleagues had a chance to contribute. The project finished ahead of schedule.

Afterwards, the team learned that Maya was caring for a family member and had been dealing with an exhausting commute. Her behaviour hadn't been a sign of arrogance or a lack of interest; she had simply been trying to manage her energy.

The experience changed how the team approached new colleagues. They began asking questions rather than drawing conclusions from limited evidence. As one colleague put it, “We weren't wrong to notice that she was quiet. We were wrong to think we knew why.”

🔎ObservationMaya is quiet in meetings.
⚖️AssumptionShe must dislike her colleagues.

Discuss: Is it fair to judge somebody based on their behaviour at work? What would you have done differently?
✦
GRAMMAR IN CONTEXT · 8 MIN

How sure are you, really?

Compare the statements. Which sound certain, and which leave room for doubt?

Strong conclusion

She must be exhausted.

They can't know the full story.

Based on evidence, we feel very confident.
Possibility or caution

She might be under pressure.

She seems to prefer quiet spaces.

We acknowledge uncertainty.
Use must for a strong positive deduction, can't for a strong negative deduction, might / may / could for possibility, and seem / appear / tend to to make interpretations less absolute.

Teacher notes and common mistakes

Emphasise that “must” expresses deduction, not obligation, in these examples. “She can't be unfriendly” means the speaker believes it is impossible, not that she is forbidden to be unfriendly. With seem, use seems to be, not seems being.

✎

Say it with more nuance

Complete the sentences with the most natural words from the box: must · might · can't · seems · tend.

Now change these direct statements into more thoughtful observations: “He's lazy.” / “She doesn't care.” / “They're unfriendly.”

CRITICAL THINKING · 6 MIN

What's the evidence?

Choose an interpretation, explain your reasoning, and suggest what extra information you would need.

Situation 1 — The unanswered message

You sent an important message to a friend yesterday. They have been online several times, but they haven't replied.

Situation 2 — The new manager

Your new manager gives very little feedback and rarely smiles during meetings. Some colleagues say they are dissatisfied with everyone's performance.

1What alternative explanations can you think of?
2What would you say to someone who jumps to conclusions?
Teacher notes

Open-ended: there is no single correct option in Situation 1. Strong responses compare evidence, propose alternative explanations and acknowledge uncertainty.

SPEAKING CHALLENGE · 7 MIN

The interview that changed everything

Role-play a conversation between two colleagues. One has formed an unfair opinion about a new team member. The other challenges that assumption politely.

🗣️Role A — Concerned colleague

You think the new colleague is not interested in the team. Explain which behaviours led you to this conclusion.

🤝Role B — Thoughtful colleague

Offer alternative explanations. Ask questions and help your colleague reconsider their assumptions.

Try to use at least four: jump to conclusions · get the wrong impression · come across as · give someone the benefit of the doubt · might · must · seem · tend to

Teacher notes

Role-play 4 minutes; switch roles for 2 minutes; feedback 1 minute. Evaluate fluency, interaction, lexical range and appropriate hedging. Save feedback for the end rather than constantly interrupting.

REVIEW & HOMEWORK · 3 MIN

What changed your mind?

Reflect on your language today. Then complete the homework before the next lesson.

1What was your most useful new expression?
2How can you disagree with an assumption without sounding rude?
Homework · 15–20 minutes

A. Vocabulary (5 minutes): Write one original sentence using each of these expressions: jump to conclusions, come across as, give someone the benefit of the doubt, challenge an assumption.

B. Grammar (5 minutes): Rewrite the sentences more cautiously: (1) “He is arrogant.” (2) “She hates her new job.” (3) “They have no experience.” (4) “He's definitely upset.”

C. Writing (10 minutes): Write 140–170 words about a time when a first impression turned out to be wrong. Explain what you assumed, what happened, what you learned, and how you would act differently. Use at least five target expressions.

Optional speaking: Record a 60-second answer to “Should we always trust our intuition about people?”

Suggested homework answers & evaluation

Sample rewrites: “He might come across as arrogant.” “She doesn't seem to enjoy her new job.” “They may not have much experience.” “He might be upset, but it's hard to tell.” Assess writing for relevance, organization, lexical range, grammatical range and accuracy. Suggested rubric: five points each for fluency/coherence, grammar, vocabulary and interaction (speaking); writing separately assessed.
